## 1. Executive Summary and Soil Organic Carbon Relationships

Soil biodiversity encompasses the variety of living organisms in the soil, ranging from microscopic bacteria and fungi to nematodes, micro-arthropods, earthworms, and subterranean mammals. These biological communities are responsible for executing vital ecosystem services, including nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, soil structure maintenance, and organic matter decomposition.

Soil organic carbon (SOC) represents the primary chemical energy reserve supporting underground biological networks. The abundance, diversity, and catalytic activity of heterotrophic soil organisms are intimately coupled to continuous inputs of decomposable organic carbon compounds.

## 2. Soil Organic Carbon as the Primary Driver of Soil Biodiversity

Scientific evidence syntheses demonstrate that soils with elevated levels of soil organic carbon (SOC > 2.0%) maintain significantly greater total microbial biomass carbon and fungal-to-bacterial biomass ratios than depleted agricultural soils where intensive tillage has reduced SOC beneath 0.8%.

A persistent depletion of soil organic matter triggers a cascading decline across trophic levels:
1. Microflora starvation: Saprophytic bacteria and mycorrhizal fungi decline as carbon substrate availability falls.
2. Mesofauna collapse: Bacterial-feeding and fungal-feeding nematodes exhibit population collapses in carbon-poor substrates.
3. Macrofauna reduction: Lumbricidae (earthworms) and burrowing coleopterans abandon low-carbon soils, impairing biogenic macropore formation and soil aeration.

Restoring organic carbon via cover cropping, residue retention, and biochar addition stimulates microbial enzymatic secretions, promoting aggregate stability and moisture retention.

## 3. Soil pH and Microbial Community Structure

Soil pH is an essential edaphic variable governing microbial biogeography and enzyme kinetics. Across global soil biomes, the optimal soil pH for general microbial respiration, organic phosphorus mineralization, and bacterial diversity ranges from pH 6.0 to 7.5.

Severe soil acidification (soil pH < 5.0), frequently caused by prolonged application of ammonium-based fertilizers or atmospheric acid deposition, causes fundamental taxonomic shifts:
- Bacterial richness declines sharply due to aluminum toxicity and proton stress.
- Acidotolerant fungal populations become dominant, decreasing the overall functional redundancy of the microbiome.
- Symbiotic nitrogen-fixing Rhizobia bacteria undergo physiological inhibition at pH < 5.5, suppressing legume nodulation and biological nitrogen fixation.

Conversely, strongly alkaline soils (soil pH > 8.2) experience micronutrient immobilization, notably reducing available iron, zinc, and phosphorus, which suppresses microbial enzyme synthesis.

## 4. Soil Moisture Dynamics and Biological Activity

Soil moisture, expressed as volumetric water content or water potential, governs both substrate diffusion and oxygen gas exchange in the soil porous network.

Under drought conditions and soil moisture depletion below the permanent wilting point (-1.5 MPa):
- Soluble organic carbon diffusion through soil pores ceases, causing microbial starvation and cellular desiccation.
- Microbial extracellular enzyme activity is inhibited, halting nitrogen mineralization.
- Earthworms and burrowing organisms migrate downward into subsoil horizons or enter diapause.

Conversely, water saturation and waterlogging for extended intervals create anaerobic conditions, suppressing aerobic decomposition while accelerating denitrification and methane emissions. Optimal biological respiration typically occurs when soil moisture is maintained between 50% and 70% of field capacity.
