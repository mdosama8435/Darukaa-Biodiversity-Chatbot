## 1. Land Degradation Processes Under Climate Change

Land degradation manifests as a persistent reduction or loss in biological productivity, ecological integrity, and soil fertility of terrestrial ecosystems. Climate change acts as a prominent stress multiplier, accelerating soil loss through enhanced rainfall erosivity, amplified thermal regimes, and prolonged drought intervals.

Degradation is closely intertwined with unsustainable land-use patterns, including continuous monoculture farming, overgrazing on marginal pastures, and deforestation. When natural vegetative cover is cleared, soil surfaces are directly exposed to kinetic raindrop impact, destroying surface aggregates and precipitating soil crusting and compaction.

## 2. Rainfall Intensity, Drought, and Soil Erosion Dynamics

Shifts in precipitation patterns exert pronounced influences on soil hydrologic function and erosion vulnerability:
- High-intensity rainfall events: When rainfall intensity exceeds the saturated hydraulic conductivity of degraded soils, excess surface runoff initiates severe rill and gully erosion, transporting topsoil, particulate organic carbon, and bioavailable nutrients into downstream aquatic systems.
- Prolonged drought intervals: Extended periods of precipitation deficit deplete root-zone soil moisture, causing widespread vegetative dieback. Loss of root tensile strength and surface vegetative canopy destabilizes topsoil horizons, leaving dried soil vulnerable to wind detachment and dust storm events.
- Soil moisture feedbacks: Depleted soil moisture alters land-surface energy partitioning by increasing sensible heat flux at the expense of latent heat flux, which elevates local near-surface temperatures and intensifies regional drying trends.

## 3. Temperature Stress, Soil Carbon Loss, and Vegetation Vulnerability

Elevated ambient temperatures compound soil degradation by accelerating heterotrophic respiration rates:
- Thermal oxidation: Increased surface temperatures accelerate the enzymatic decomposition of soil organic carbon, turning managed terrestrial soils from net carbon sinks into net atmospheric carbon sources.
- Desiccation and microbial mortality: Sustained thermal stress dries surface horizons, restricting bacterial and fungal communities to microrefugia deeper in the pedon and decreasing mycorrhizal fungal colonization in host plants.
- Species richness decline: The concurrent combination of high temperature stress and soil moisture deficits reduces vascular plant species richness, simplifies community composition, and increases ecosystem susceptibility to degradation and woody invasive species establishment.

Sustainable land management practices, such as agroforestry, rotational grazing, and vegetative buffer strips, provide thermal insulation and reduce runoff velocity, thereby preserving soil carbon and protecting biodiversity.
